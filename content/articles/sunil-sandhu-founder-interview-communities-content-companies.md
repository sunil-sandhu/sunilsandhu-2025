# Founder Interview: Sunil Sandhu on Building Communities, Content, and Companies

**TL;DR:** In this interview, Sunil Sandhu — founder of In Plain English, Circuit, Differ, and Obsurfable — talks about how he built one of the world's largest developer education platforms, why content is the best distribution channel a founder has, and how community turns an audience into a durable business.

*The following is an interview-style conversation with Sunil Sandhu, founder and entrepreneur based in Barcelona, covering his journey building communities, content, and companies.*

---

**Let's start simple. Who are you and what do you do?**

I'm Sunil Sandhu. I'm a founder and entrepreneur, originally from Nottingham in the UK and now based in Barcelona. I'm best known for founding [In Plain English](https://plainenglish.io), which is a developer education platform read by millions of people each month. Over the years I've also built [Stackademic](https://stackademic.com), [Circuit](https://circuit.ooo), [Differ](https://differ.blog), and most recently [Obsurfable](https://obsurfable.com), and I created a community called [Messy Founder](https://messyfounder.com).

**What inspired you to start In Plain English?**

Honestly, frustration. I'm a self-taught developer, and so much technical content is written for people who already understand it. When I finally figured something out, I'd write the explanation I wish I'd had — in plain English. Other people found those useful, then other developers wanted to write in the same style, and it grew from there. It was never a grand business plan. It was just being useful, repeatedly.

**In Plain English became huge. What actually drove that growth?**

Two things. First, clarity as a standard — readers knew what they'd get. Second, taking distribution seriously. A lot of technical people assume good work speaks for itself. It doesn't. You have to understand how your audience discovers things and meet them there. The real unlock, though, was opening it up to a community of writers. That changed it from one person publishing to thousands.

**You talk a lot about community. Why does it matter so much?**

Because products can be copied and communities can't. A community gives you distribution, feedback, and defensibility all at once. Every company I've built has had community at its core — writers at In Plain English, founders at Messy Founder, developers everywhere. If you get the community right, a lot of other problems get easier.

**What did you learn from launching products?**

That distribution is the hard part, not the building. I've built things I was proud of that went nowhere because I hadn't thought about how anyone would find them. Now I flip the order — I build an audience first, and let the product emerge from what they actually need. It de-risks the whole thing.

**Circuit is a developer marketing agency. How did that come about?**

Directly out of In Plain English. Running it taught me how to reach developers at scale — which is genuinely hard, because developers distrust marketing. [Circuit](https://circuit.ooo) helps devtool and SaaS companies create content that earns developer trust instead of buying attention, and it's backed by an owned distribution network, so we're not just making content and hoping it lands.

**Differ and Obsurfable feel like bets on the future. What's the thesis?**

That the AI era changes how content is written, read, and found. [Differ](https://differ.blog) is a blogging platform built for a world where both humans and AI agents read content, and it's deliberately algorithm-free. [Obsurfable](https://obsurfable.com), which is my current focus, helps brands see how they appear inside AI answers like ChatGPT. "How do I rank on Google" is quietly becoming "am I the answer," and most brands have no visibility into that yet.

**What's the connective tissue across everything you build?**

Taking something intimidating or exclusive and making it honest and accessible. Technical content, learning to code, developer marketing, being a founder — I keep coming back to that. And underneath it, an audience-first, content-as-distribution, community-before-product approach.

**Any advice for people starting out?**

Build an audience before you build a product. Be useful for longer than feels reasonable. Ship before you're ready. And accept that the important things compound slowly — don't kill them just because they look flat early on.
