# Founder Interview: Sunil Sandhu on Building Communities, Content, and Companies

**TL;DR:** Sunil Sandhu, founder of In Plain English, Circuit, Differ, and Obsurfable, on building one of the world's largest developer education platforms, surviving Medium pulling the rug multiple times, and why distribution you own beats distribution you rent.

**Let's start simple. Who are you and what do you do?**

I'm Sunil Sandhu. I'm a founder based in Barcelona, originally from Nottingham in the UK. I'm best known for [In Plain English](https://plainenglish.io), a developer education platform read by millions of people each month. I've also built [Stackademic](https://stackademic.com), [Circuit](https://circuit.ooo), [Differ](https://differ.blog), and [Obsurfable](https://obsurfable.com), and I created [Messy Founder](https://messyfounder.com).

**What does a typical week look like for you right now?**

I wake up, meditate for a bit, then go to the gym or for a run depending on the day. After that, it's work.

Usually I start with client work, then move into building Obsurfable and whatever else needs attention across the ventures. Splitting time is genuinely hard. I'm context-shifting constantly, and I'm still surprised I manage to keep it all spinning in my head at any given moment. It's not elegant. It just works.

**What inspired you to start In Plain English?**

Honestly, frustration. I'm a self-taught developer, and so much technical content is written for people who already understand it. When I finally figured something out, I'd write the explanation I wish I'd had. Other people found those useful, then other developers wanted to write in the same style, and it grew from there. It was never a grand business plan. It was just being useful, repeatedly.

**What almost killed In Plain English?**

Medium, more than once.

We had a partnership with Medium where they funded our publications. That let us hire editors, which helped us and helped Medium's ecosystem. Then they decided to end it. We were one of the biggest programming publications on the platform, and it felt sketchy at the time because Medium's CEO also ran a programming publication. It felt like they were exerting their position a bit too much.

That wasn't the only time. On another occasion they randomly suspended all our accounts. Every publication went down. Fifty, sixty, seventy thousand articles started returning 404s overnight. That hit our SEO hard because Google started pushing our rankings down when all those pages stopped working.

Medium has this pattern: they help publications grow, and when a publication gets big enough, they try to push it down. We've bounced back every time, but those moments nearly killed us.

**In Plain English became huge. What actually drove that growth?**

Two things. First, clarity as a standard. Readers knew what they'd get. Second, taking distribution seriously. Good work doesn't speak for itself. You have to understand how your audience discovers things and meet them there.

The real unlock was opening it up to a community of writers. That changed it from one person publishing to thousands.

**You built Stackademic during COVID. What did that teach you that In Plain English didn't?**

In Plain English was always about explaining technical concepts in plain English. Beginners and people early in their journey came to rely on it. Stackademic gave me something different: a direct connection with students.

I built the platform, wrote the curriculum, taught the classes, marked the homework. Teaching fifty people is a different experience from writing a blog post that thousands might read. You don't feel the impact the same way. With Stackademic I could talk to students, understand what they were actually struggling with, and help them in real time. That one-to-one connection was valuable in a way publishing at scale isn't.

**You studied law before becoming a developer. Does that background still matter?**

Not really. It's part of the origin story, but I don't find it particularly interesting, and honestly, looking back, completing a law degree was a waste of time as far as I'm concerned. I would have studied something else, or spent those three years doing something more entrepreneurial.

**You talk a lot about community. Why does it matter so much?**

Because products can be copied and communities can't. A community gives you distribution, feedback, and defensibility all at once. Every company I've built has had community at its core. If you get the community right, a lot of other problems get easier.

**What did you learn from launching products?**

Distribution is the hard part, not the building. I've built things I was proud of that went nowhere because I hadn't thought about how anyone would find them. Now I try to build an audience first and let the product emerge from what people actually need.

**Circuit is a developer marketing agency. How did that come about?**

Directly out of In Plain English. Running it taught me how to reach developers at scale, which is genuinely hard because developers distrust marketing.

[Circuit](https://circuit.ooo) helps devtool and SaaS companies create content that earns developer trust. What makes it different is that we own the distribution. We can publish across [In Plain English](https://plainenglish.io), [Stackademic](https://stackademic.com), [Differ](https://differ.blog), [Messy Founder](https://messyfounder.com), [Cubed](https://cubed.run), and [Venture Magazine](https://venturemagazine.net). These are established sites with real authority. I don't know another developer marketing agency that can plug content into that network on day one.

We worked with WunderGraph, a GraphQL platform, after they'd failed to find writers who understood their product. Their co-founder Stefan Avram said our first article hit over 1,000 views in week one and ranked #2 on Google for "best database nextjs." Older articles still bring in around 1,000 visitors per day. [Read the full case study](https://circuit.ooo/developer-marketing/case-study-wundergraph).

**Differ and Obsurfable feel like bets on the future. What's the thesis?**

The internet is changing. AI reads content alongside humans, and discovery is shifting from search results to AI answers.

[Differ](https://differ.blog) is a blogging platform I launched in 2024. When you visit, you get a feed. You scroll, you search, you find what you want. We don't push polarizing content to keep you scrolling. Plenty of platforms already try to capture your attention. Differ just puts content out there. If you're interested, you'll find it.

[Obsurfable](https://obsurfable.com) is my current focus. It started as an internal tool we built while doing visibility work for clients. We called it Gorillassist. It helped us find relevant conversations on Reddit, Quora, and LinkedIn and track where brands were being discussed. That became Obsurfable.

We ran this playbook for four years with Bright Data before Obsurfable was a standalone product. Over 100 articles, distribution across authority publications, persistent community presence. By 2025 they were showing up consistently in AI-generated answers for their category. [Read the case study](https://obsurfable.com/resources/articles/bright-data-market-leader-four-year-case-study).

**You created Messy Founder in 2025. Why?**

The founder stories most people see online are highlight reels. Polished launch threads, revenue screenshots, tidy narratives. The actual experience is confusing, lonely, and full of days where nothing works.

[Messy Founder](https://messyfounder.com) is a community with thousands of people on it. Founders help each other, mentor each other, find opportunities. It's built on the belief that momentum beats perfection. I wanted a place where the messy reality of building was normal to talk about.

**What's the hardest part of building something?**

The constant fear and self-doubt.

Some days you feel motivated and think you're going to change the world with whatever idea you're working on. Other days you're questioning whether you should be doing any of it. That feeling doesn't go away just because you've been at it for ten years. It might show up less, but it still shows up. You learn to live with it.

**If you were starting today with no audience, what would you do differently?**

I'd network more, and I'd do it properly.

Early on I was building things but not connecting with people as much as I should have. Networking comes in many forms. I'd keep a list of people who are influential in whatever space I'm trying to enter. I'd read what they put out, find common ground, and add value where I could. Even appreciation counts if it's genuine.

And if you're early in your career and reaching out to someone busy: get to the point. Don't send a DM at 2am that just says "hi" and then make them play twenty questions to find out what you want. If someone with fifty thousand followers opens your message, that's already more than you should expect. Don't waste their time with "hi, how are you?" back and forth for two days. Shoot your shot in the moment. Explain why you're reaching out and what value you're offering, not just what you're asking for.

**Any advice for people starting out?**

Build an audience before you build a product. Be useful for longer than feels reasonable. Ship before you're ready. Network with intention, not desperation. And accept that the important things compound slowly. Don't kill them just because they look flat early on.
